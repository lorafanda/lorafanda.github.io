Overview

Where the analysis stands

Intracranial (sEEG/depth + micro) recordings during a three‑condition language task — picture naming, auditory naming, and reading completion — are turned into one time‑warped time–frequency substrate, then examined through three complementary lenses: unsupervised clustering, supervised decoding, and confirmatory a‑priori pooling. The scientific arc

The stages answer a chain of questions — each set up by the one before:

  • Q1 · Can we cluster language? → Not cleanly. Data‑driven clustering gives only weak separation — partly the task design, partly that language isn't one separable process but a distributed, overlapping set. "Find the language cluster" fails by design.
  • Q2 · So are the clusters random? → No. They track the condition (audio / picture / reading) — a real modality/sensory axis, not noise. (Because clustering keeps conditions as separate samples, the condition contrast is the strongest structure available to find.)
  • Q3 · Does supervised decoding do better — and which target? → Condition beats network. The condition is readable from a single electrode well above chance; its Yeo network is much harder. The modality signal is strong and decodable; the anatomical‑network signal is faint.
  • Q4 · Why does condition decode — which features separate the classes? → The early, modality‑specific response. The logistic‑regression weights concentrate in the early stimulus window (before the GO‑cue), where the conditions differ by input (auditory vs visual); the shared speech response afterwards carries little condition information. (see stage 03)

Stage 04 then steps back from data‑driven grouping to a‑priori roles and the response‑timing sequence — the "what fires when, and where" that clustering couldn't isolate.

The five figures below answer those four questions in order, one figure per argument. Each gathers the panels belonging to a single claim so it can be judged in one place; the stage tabs hold the same panels with the diagnostics behind them.

How it was built

Everything above rests on these four stages. This is the method, not the argument.

  • 01 · Signal → ERSP. Raw clinical recordings → cleaned, re‑referenced signals → trial‑locked, baseline‑normalised ERSP cubes (129 freq × 300 warped time) per electrode × condition. Feeds everything below.
  • 02 · Clustering — unsupervised: "what structure emerges?" Let the data group the ERSPs into response types, no labels given.
  • 03 · Decoding — supervised: "what can we read out?" Test whether condition, and separately Yeo network, can be predicted from one electrode's response.
  • 04 · Pooling — confirmatory: "where are the a‑priori roles, and when?" Match hypothesis‑driven functional roles and read their response‑timing on the warped timeline.

How the stages relate & compare

  • Same data, three questions. All three read the identical ERSP substrate; they differ only in who supplies the structure — the data (clustering), the labels (decoding), or prior theory (pooling).
  • They cross‑check each other. What the unsupervised clustering surfaces on its own should be the same axis the supervised decoder can exploit, and the a‑priori roles should land where both put their signal. Agreement across lenses is the evidence; disagreement flags where the story is fragile.
  • Different sample units, and the canonical track changed. The clustering run every figure here uses (kmeans/concat_hg 20260803_175417) concatenates the three conditions into one 900‑bin per‑electrode profile — 1027 electrodes — exactly as decoding‑parcellation and pooling do. The older per‑condition track, which kept each electrode × condition as its own sample (2102 samples, K=9), is retained only for comparison. The sample unit still sets what each lens can ask; it is no longer what distinguishes them.

Method — notebook 140 (the ERSP producer)

  • Load & clean. Format‑agnostic (TRC/EDF/H5); drop aux; white‑matter re‑reference (subtract mean of ≥3 good WM contacts, macros only).
  • Adaptive mains notch. 50 Hz + harmonics, but a harmonic is notched only if its spectral peak z ≥ 3 over local background; zero‑phase.
  • Trial QC. Hard filters (min stim 0.5 s, max post 10 s) then IQR outlier trim (k=1.5).
  • ERSP. Resample 1 kHz → STFT (Hann, nperseg=128, nfft=256, ~85% overlap) → dB. Time‑warped "TN": baseline/stim/post onto a 300‑bin axis, stimulus and response each 50% (bin 150 = GO‑cue). Baseline window (−0.6, −0.1) s; display ±6 dB, 0–500 Hz.
  • High‑gamma. 70–150 Hz Butterworth → Hilbert envelope → 25 ms smoothing → baseline z.
  • Output. Per‑channel ERSP .npy (in 04_ersp_LM_RAWONLY/…/ERSP_matrix/) — the cube 02/03/04 read.

Choosing K — and why only one of the three can answer it

The held-out variance curve that was on this page holds out electrodes only, then refits each held-out electrode its own loadings across the full feature set. Adding a basis vector can only reduce a least-squares residual, so that score is monotone in K by construction — on concat_hg it climbed 0.389 to 0.674 and never turned over. choose_k.py says so in its own docstring. A monotone curve cannot choose K and cannot compare methods.

Bi-cross-validation fixes it. A block of ROWS and a block of COLUMNS is held out; the method is fitted on the remaining block; each held-out electrode's loadings come from the TRAIN columns only and are scored on the TEST columns. The loadings never see the values they are graded on, so an extra component has to earn its place. The only thing that differs between the three methods is how those loadings are obtained — NNLS against the components for convex NMF, nearest centroid for k-means and Ward.

feature setconvex NMFk-meansWard

Convex NMF is the only method whose curve turns over anywhere in K = 5…30. That is not a quirk: a hard partition does not self-limit here, because more centroids always quantise the space more finely and so always predict a held-out block better. Only the graded model pays for an extra component. So K is read from convex NMF and then applied to all three — k-means' and Ward's argmax would simply be "the largest K in the sweep".

K legitimately differs between feature sets (11 vs 12). Same electrodes, different representation; there is no reason for one number to serve both.

Response timing — when does each cluster come on?

Stage-04 pooling asks "when does each a-priori functional role come on, and in what order?" (FIG 4.2 / 4.3). This asks the same question of the data-driven clusters. The distinction is the point: roles are hypothesis-driven and fixed, so their ordering tests prior theory; clusters are discovered, so their ordering describes what the data separates. If the two agree, the roles are recovering real timing rather than imposing it.

The concatenated track is the only one where this is well posed. One sample = one electrode carrying [audio | picture | reading], so a cluster's three onsets belong to the same electrodes. In the per-condition tracks an "audio cluster" and a "reading cluster" contain different contacts, so their onsets are not comparable.

  • Onset is defined identically to pooling — first bin whose |mean HGA| crosses 0.5 dB, as % of the warped trial. That is deliberate: it is what lets the cluster ladder and the role ladder be read on one axis.
  • Split into two windows. A single "first crossing anywhere" conflates a sensory response before the GO cue with a production response after it. Reported separately: ● stimulus onset (0–50%) and ▲ response onset (50–100%). A window with no crossing is not plotted — a cluster silent before GO has no stimulus onset, and drawing it at 100 would invent a very late sensory response.
  • Hollow ▲ = carry-over. A response onset on the first post-GO bin nearly always means the signal was already above threshold at the cue and simply continued. Without the flag those read as "fires exactly at GO", which is the opposite of what happened. At K=5, 5 of 15 cells are carry-over and one (c1 / audio) has no response onset at all, so 9 are genuinely new response-locked onsets.
  • Two inherited caveats. The criterion uses |mean|, so a strongly suppressed cluster registers an onset exactly like an activated one — at K=5 only c2 (n=130) is a suppression cluster, negative in all three conditions (peak −1.31 / −0.84 / −1.53); c3 and c4 are activations. Since 2026-08-08 polarity is a stored column and suppression is marked on both ladders. And onset is a threshold crossing on a warped axis, so the ordering is the result; the exact percentages are not.

Across the K sweep — and why the obvious version of this figure is misleading

The natural next question is "is this timing ordering stable, or an artefact of the K we picked?" The obvious figure — every cluster's onset plotted against K, with a median line — cannot answer it, and worse, it looks like it can. Three specific problems, each now addressed in its own row of FIG 2.12.

  • 1 · It had no notion of identity. "Stable ordering" is a claim about which cluster is which, and a scatter of unlabelled dots has no identity — you cannot tell which cluster at K=5 became which at K=6. Fixed by matching clusters between consecutive K by maximum sample overlap (Jaccard), giving each a lineage that persists across the sweep. Median link strength 0.774. Only 4 lineages survive from K=4, which is itself informative — the sweep is subdividing four groups, not finding twenty.
  • 2 · It showed a size artefact as if it were biology. This is the serious one. A smaller cluster has a noisier mean, and a noisier mean crosses a fixed dB threshold sooner. So as K grows and clusters shrink, onsets drift earlier — for statistical reasons, not neural ones. Measured on this run: Spearman(cluster n, stimulus onset) = +0.50 audio, +0.68 picture, +0.41 reading (recomputed after the 33 non-detections became NaN), all p < 1e-7. That is a first-order effect, not a nuisance. Any reading of "the spread grows with K" has to be discounted against it, so it now gets its own panel instead of being invisible.
  • 3 · It hid its own denominator. Clusters with no onset were silently dropped, so a K where half the clusters are silent looked sparse rather than uninformative. Coverage is now plotted — and it bites: in picture only 40–60% of clusters have any stimulus-window onset at all, against 80–100% in audio and reading. The old figure gave no hint of that.
What can honestly be claimed. Given the size confound, "the onset ordering is stable across K" is not a defensible finding from this figure. What is defensible: the ordering at a fixed K, stated with the confound, plus the one result that survives every K in the top three — a large cluster (n=546–633) that is silent in audio and late everywhere else, sitting opposite a small cluster (n=70–125) that responds early. To make a genuinely K-robust claim the cluster-size effect has to be removed by construction — computing onset on equal-n bootstrap subsamples of each cluster would do it. Not yet run.

Supporting checks — reproducibility, K choice, and method agreement

Four checks that sit behind the results above. None is a headline; together they are what makes the headline safe to state.

Cross-correlation — do the clusters lead and lag each other?

Section 2.11 asks when each cluster comes on. This asks whether they are temporally distinguishable: one cluster is fixed as the reference — the auditory cluster — and every other cluster's mean HG trace is correlated against it across time shifts, giving a full r vs lag curve.

  • Sign. r(τ) = Pearson[ ref(t), other(t+τ) ]; τ > 0 means that cluster lags behind the reference. Verified on synthetic data in both directions. Lags are in warped-time %, so they are an ordering, not a latency in ms.
  • Response window only, and that is not a preference. Cross-correlation is only meaningful when two signals are the same shape shifted in time. Over the full trial these cluster means are not — one has an early sensory transient, another only a late production bump. Checked against the actual difference in peak times: full trial 1 of 9, response window 8 of 9 (agreement = same sign and within 15% of the peak-time difference; the exact counts move with that tolerance, the gap between the two windows does not). There are nine non-reference clusters at K=10 — this line used to say eight. The full-trial version is computed and shown in the notebook purely to demonstrate the failure.
  • Every lag carries its own caveat. peak_r says whether the shapes align at all (below 0.3 in absolute value the lag is not a delay — a suppression aligns at r ≈ −1, which is a perfect alignment, not the absence of one), the bootstrap CI over electrodes says whether the lag is resolvable, at_edge flags a lag censored at the search boundary, and anti flags a pair whose polarities differ, for which the offset is not a lead or a lag at all. Curves failing any of these are drawn dashed. 18 of 27 pass — 22 under the old estimator, and this line used to claim 25.
The obvious null does not work, and it is worth saying why. Shuffling cluster labels and testing the peak correlation gives p = 1.000 — shuffling makes both groups random samples of the same population, so their means collapse toward the grand mean and correlate at ~0.97. Shuffling and testing the lag does work (the null lag is ~0) but is weak, because a null of essentially zero width is beaten by almost any real lag. The bootstrap CI, not the p-value, is the statistic to read.

The Clustering Visualizer — turning a caveat into a measurement

Every brain figure above carries the same footnote: dot density reflects where electrodes were implanted, not where language is. It is written in prose, which means nobody can check it. This section is the methods write-up for the tool that turns that sentence into a quantity you can look at, threshold, and export — clustering_visualizer.

The problem, stated plainly.
  • A cluster's glassbrain shows where its contacts are. It cannot show where its contacts could have been. A region with no dots is either genuinely uninvolved or simply never implanted, and the figure looks identical either way.
  • So the tool computes two different things and keeps them apart: how well a piece of cortex was sampled, and what the electrodes there were doing. The first is the denominator of the second.

1 · Why the existing projection could not be reused

The activity visualizer already projects contacts onto fsaverage, so the obvious move was to reuse it. That projection keeps, for each vertex, only the k = 4 nearest contacts. A patient count taken from it is therefore bounded:

np(v) ≤ k = 4   ⇒   P̂samp(v) ≤ 424 = 0.167 (1)

A "probability of being sampled" that can never exceed 0.17 out of 24 patients is not a probability, it is an artefact of the projection. Everything below therefore uses a fresh, radius-based count in which every contact inside the neighbourhood is counted.

2 · The neighbourhood, and the three counts

Let C be the set of reconstructed contacts, xi the fsaverage coordinate of contact i, and π(i) the patient it belongs to. For a cortical vertex v the neighbourhood is a closed ball of radius r = 10 mm:

Nr(v) = { i ∈ C : ‖ xi − v ‖2 ≤ r } (2)

Three counts are stored per vertex. The first two describe sampling and are identical for every clustering run; the third is the only part that depends on how the electrodes were labelled.

nc(v) = | Nr(v) | (3)
np(v) = | { π(i) : i ∈ Nr(v) } | (4)
nk(v) = Σi∈Nr(v) Σs∈S(i) 𝟙[ c(s) = k ] (5)

The inner sum is not decoration. S(i) is the set of label rows belonging to contact i. In the concatenated runs each electrode is one row and the sum collapses. In the per-condition runs a row is an electrode × condition sample, and an electrode can be assigned to a different cluster for audio than for reading — between 328 and 394 electrodes per run do exactly that. Writing this as a one-label-per-electrode lookup silently keeps whichever row happened to come last; that bug was live in the first build and would have produced a wrong map for four of the seven runs. The unit is recorded in the manifest and stated in the readout.

m(v) = Σk nk(v)    (labelled samples in the neighbourhood) (6)

3 · Two estimators, two denominators

With P = 24 patients contributing 3506 localised contacts (the concatenated cohort; the per-condition cohort has 26 and 3836), the tool draws exactly two families of map. They differ only in what they divide by — which is the whole point, and why the bundle ships counts rather than ratios: a stored ratio hides its own denominator.

P̂samp(v) = np(v)P (7)
P̂( k | v ) = nk(v)m(v) (8)
  • (7) is a coverage map. "Of the patients in this run’s cohort, how many have an electrode within 10 mm of here?" It does not depend on any clustering, so it is computed once and shared by every run. Observed maximum: 20 of 24 patients at one vertex, i.e. P̂ = 0.83.
  • (8) is a conditional, not a density. "Given that an electrode is here, how likely is it to belong to cluster k?" A high value means the cortex is enriched for that cluster relative to its overall share — it never means the cluster lives only there.
Which patients count. The coordinate file carries 35 patients, but only 26 ever entered an FBM run — 24 in the concatenated cohort, 26 in the per-condition one. Nine patients have reconstructed contacts and appear in no run.
  • Counting them inflated both sides of every ratio: 5247 contacts where the concatenated cohort has 3506, over 35 patients where it has 24.
  • Coverage is therefore computed per cohort, over that cohort's contacts, against that cohort's patient count. Runs are grouped by patient set, so this costs one extra coverage map rather than one per run.
  • The corrected figures are the ones quoted above. The earlier 87/81% and P̂ = 0.80 were computed against the wrong denominator.

4 · Why a minimum count, and what grey means

Equation (8) is a binomial proportion. Its standard error is

SE[ P̂(k|v) ] = √ P̂(1 − P̂)m(v) (9)

which is pathological at small m: with m = 1 the estimate is exactly 0 or exactly 1 and (9) returns zero — maximal apparent confidence from a single contact. Rendering that would put saturated colour on the least reliable vertices in the brain. So a vertex is drawn only if

m(v) ≥ nmin = 5   (cluster maps)      np(v) ≥ 1   (coverage maps) (10)

Everything failing (10) is drawn grey, and grey means "no estimate" — never "probability zero". That distinction is the entire reason the tool exists, so it is stated in the readout panel on every map rather than left to a caption. The consequence is visible in the numbers: 81% of cortex lies within 10 mm of some electrode (82.5% left, 79.4% right), but only ~18% survives the 5-sample gate on a K=5 cluster map.

5 · Rendering: scale and opacity

A cluster holding 7% of the data has most of its map near zero and a few vertices near one. Mapped naively onto 0–1, it renders as flat purple with a handful of saturated specks — the specks being precisely the 5-of-5 vertices with the weakest evidence. The colour ceiling is therefore set from the distribution rather than the extreme, taking the 98th percentile over gated vertices:

Pmax = Q0.98 { P̂(k|v) : m(v) ≥ nmin } ,    t(v) = min( 1, P̂(k|v) / Pmax ) (11)

Colour is then a blend from the anatomy grey g toward viridis, with opacity rising as a power of t so that low probabilities stay close to plain anatomy and the eye is not drawn to them:

α(t) = α0 + (1 − α0) tγ ,    α0 = 0.15 ,   γ = 0.8 (12)
c(v) = (1 − α) g + α · viridis( t(v) ) (13)

viridis is sequential and perceptually uniform. A diverging map (the bwr used on the activity page) would be wrong here: a probability has no meaningful midpoint to diverge about. Because (11) is a chosen scale rather than the true range, the slider visibly moves to Pmax and the readout names the value — the scale is stated, not hidden.

6 · Storage: what made seven runs affordable

Storing all counts together for every run costs, per hemisphere with V = 163842 vertices, K clusters and b bytes per count:

Bdense = R · V ( 2 + K ) b   →   Bsplit = 2V b + Σj=1..R V Kj b (14)

The underlined term is paid once. Sampling depends only on electrode geometry, so nc and np are identical across runs — the same fact that makes (7) run-independent also makes it storable once. Separately, every count fits in one byte, which was checked rather than assumed:

max nc = 148 ,   max np = 20 ,   max nk = 37   <   256  ⇒  b = 1 (15)

The writer falls back to uint16 and records the dtype in the manifest, so a larger radius cannot silently wrap around. Together the two changes took one run from 4.95 MB to 0.66 MB shared + ~1.6 MB per run; seven runs total 20 MB, of which the page fetches only the selected run.

Runs bundled — the newest of each usable track, in two cohorts. Cross-cohort comparison is possible but flagged, because mixing cohorts silently is exactly the problem the 1027-electrode cleanup fixed.

  • 2026-07-19 per-task cohort (unit = electrode×condition; 2102 rows → 1287 localised contacts → 2031 labelled samples): K-means K=9 · Ward K=13 · K-means 15-band K=11 · Ward 15-band K=10.

9 · Limitations

  • The radius is a smoothing kernel. r = 10 mm is a boxcar: every contact inside contributes equally and every contact outside not at all. It blurs sharp functional borders and cannot resolve structure finer than the ball.
  • Neighbourhoods overlap, so vertices are not independent. The maps are a description of this cohort's sampling, not a test. No inference should be drawn from the spatial extent of a coloured patch.
  • The gate trades coverage for reliability. At nmin = 5 roughly 18% of cortex is shown on a cluster map. Small clusters can vanish entirely — correctly, but the absence is a property of the sampling, not of the brain.
  • Depth contacts are projected to a surface. A contact in white matter or deep in a sulcus is counted toward nearby surface vertices, which flatters coverage in regions sampled mainly by depth electrodes.
  • (8) conditions on being an electrode in this dataset. It inherits every selection effect upstream — clinical implantation targets, the electrode-inclusion criteria, and the excluded shafts — and is not a statement about cortex in general.

Graded decomposition vs classic partition — the full accounting

The clustering track moved from a hard partition (k-means / Ward: every electrode gets exactly one label) to a graded decomposition (convex NMF: every electrode gets a weight on each of K components). This section is the honest ledger for that decision — what the change buys, what it costs, and where the comparison as usually presented flatters the new method. Every number below was re-derived from the files on 2026-08-12, not quoted from an earlier write-up.

What convex NMF actually does here. Ordinary NMF needs non-negative input, which baseline-relative dB is not. Convex NMF (Ding, Li & Jordan 2010) factorises X ≈ X W G' with W, G ≥ 0 — the data itself is never required to be positive, only the weights. Each component is therefore a weighted average of real electrodes, so it stays in dB and stays interpretable, and each electrode gets a non-negative mixture over components rather than a winner-takes-all label. Hamilton et al. (2021) use the same family on human sTG.

0 — The equations, and how membership is actually decided

Written out because "graded decomposition" is doing a lot of work in the sentences above, and because the step people expect — the one where an electrode is assigned to a component — does not exist.

The factorisation. X is n electrodes × m features — for concat_hg, 1266 × 900, the three conditions' 300-bin high-gamma traces laid end to end, in dB against the pre-stimulus baseline, and therefore signed. The model is

X  ≈  G (W′ X)     with   W ≥ 0,   G ≥ 0
C  =  W′ X    (K × m)  — the component profiles
columns of W are normalised to sum to 1
  • W (n × K) builds the components out of the electrodes themselves. Because each column is non-negative and sums to one, component j is a convex combination — a weighted average — of real observed responses. That is the whole point of the "convex" variant: the component keeps the units of the data, so it can be plotted as a dB response profile and read directly, rather than being an abstract direction in feature space.
  • G (n × K) is the membership, and it is graded by construction. G[i, j] is how much electrode i expresses component j. Every electrode has a value for every component. Nothing forces one of them to win.

Why the sign of the data is the reason for this method. The updates never touch X directly, only the Gram matrix A = X X′, which is split into its positive and negative parts:

A⁺ = (|A| + A) / 2     A⁻ = (|A| − A) / 2    (both ≥ 0)

W ← W ⊙ sqrt[ (A⁺G + A⁻W G′G) / (A⁻G + A⁺W G′G) ]
G ← G ⊙ sqrt[ (A⁺W + G W′A⁻W) / (A⁻W + G W′A⁺W) ]

Only A⁺ and A⁻ enter, and both are non-negative, so a suppression (negative dB) is modelled as itself rather than being clipped, shifted or split into a separate "negative" component. The updates are multiplicative: W and G start positive and are only ever multiplied by positive ratios, so non-negativity is preserved without any projection or constraint step. Convergence is on ‖X − G W′X‖F, checked every 10 iterations, relative tolerance 1e−6, at most 300 iterations.

It is seeded from k-means, and that matters for how you read it. G is initialised from a k-means partition at the same K (1.0 on the assigned cluster, 0.2 elsewhere, plus noise) and W from the column-normalised G. So the decomposition does not discover structure independently of k-means — it relaxes the k-means solution, letting electrodes that the partition forced into one cluster spread their weight. When the graded fit and the partition agree, that agreement is partly inherited, not independent corroboration.

0a — So how is an electrode's component decided?

It is not. There is no assignment step anywhere in the algorithm. What exists is a weight vector per electrode, normalised so it sums to one:

Ĝ[i, j] = G[i, j] / Σj G[i, j]   — the published w0…w6
top_weight[i] = maxj Ĝ[i, j]    margin[i] = top − second

A single label only appears where a tool demands one. MOBA and the clustering visualizer need a hard colour per contact, so publish_decomposition.py writes argmaxj Ĝ[i, j] into the cluster column — and that number is a lossy summary of the thing it summarises. On the current 1266-electrode fit:

Read the argmax map as "which component leads here", never as "which type this electrode is". For two thirds of contacts the lead is a plurality, not a majority. The full w0…w6 ride along in labels.csv beside top_weight and margin, so nothing is thrown away and the uncertainty stays inspectable.

0b — How K is chosen

Not by silhouette. Silhouette rewards compact well-separated blobs and cannot get worse from over-splitting in the way that matters here, so on graded data it is close to uninformative. K is chosen on held-out reconstruction (cv_rank_curve, the logic Norman-Haignere et al. 2022 use):

  1. Split the electrodes into 5 folds.
  2. Fit the decomposition without one fold — those electrodes are never seen.
  3. Project the held-out electrodes onto the fitted components by non-negative least squares.
  4. Measure their reconstruction error.

Because the held-out electrodes had no say in the components, this can get worse when the rank is too high — which is exactly what makes it a criterion rather than a monotone reward. K = 7 is the current choice; the knee of the curve sits at 5→6 and 7 was carried forward from the validation. The curve is F3a_cv_rank.png in each decomposition/<feature_set>/ folder, and the numbers behind it are cv_rank_curve.csv beside it.

Being straight about the K choice: the knee is at 5→6 and the run uses 7. That is a defensible margin on a flat curve, but it is a judgement call, not something the criterion forced. It should be stated that way in any write-up.

1 — Why the graded decomposition beats k-means here

Not "it is a fancier method". Three specific things k-means gets wrong on this dataset, each measured rather than argued.

  • A k-means partition of this data is not reproducible. Fit the same K=5 on five preprocessing variants of the same electrodes and the partitions agree at ARI 0.085 at worst, 0.168 median (1.0 = identical, 0 = chance). At K=7, 0.107 / 0.162. The preprocessing choice, not the data, is picking the clusters. Nothing built on top of one such partition can be trusted to survive a normalisation change — and this is the single strongest argument, because it holds no matter what replaces k-means.
  • Most electrodes do not have a cluster to belong to. Under the graded fit, 66.0% have no component above 0.5 and the median largest weight is 0.432; only 1.7% are dominated by one component. k-means still hands all of them a confident label. For the majority of the dataset that label is close to a coin flip between the top two components — and nothing in the k-means output tells you which electrodes those are.
  • The graded labels line up better with anatomy. Neighbouring electrodes share a label 2.05× above chance under the graded argmax, against 1.38× for the shipped k-means (now K=6, not K=5). This is the criterion that is about the brain rather than about reconstruction error, and it is the one you said you actually care about. Read the honest version of this comparison in the caveat below — the gap is real but smaller than that pairing suggests.
  • It fits the sign of the data without a workaround. Convex NMF factorises X ≈ XWG' with only the weights constrained non-negative, so baseline-relative dB goes in unmodified and every component stays a weighted average of real electrodes in real dB units (Ding, Li & Jordan 2010; the same family Hamilton et al. 2021 use on human sTG). Note this is not an advantage over k-means, which is equally sign-agnostic — an earlier version of this page wrongly claimed it was.
  • One patient does move it — this claim has been withdrawn. On the 24-patient fit the worst leave-one-patient-out fold (ARI 0.643) sat inside a size-matched pseudo-patient null (0.725 ± 0.118, z = −0.69) and this page concluded that no individual carried the solution. At 27 patients that reverses: worst fold ARI 0.627 (EL043) against a null of 0.814 ± 0.024 — z = −7.91, well outside it. Removing that one patient does measurably more damage than removing the same number of random electrodes. concat_rawds still sits inside its own null (z = −0.52), so this is specific to the HG feature set. The shipped k-means has never been tested this way.

2 — Reading the results with a critical eye

Six things to hold in mind. The first three are places this page has previously overstated the case; the rest are properties of the method that no amount of tuning removes.

Where the comparison flatters the new method.
  • The anatomy bars compare different K. FIG C.3 panel D puts graded K=7 (2.00×) against k-means at K=5 (1.36×). Re-deriving the whole curve: k-means gives 1.418× at K=5, 1.376× at the shipped K=6, 1.580× at K=7, 1.730× at K=10. Against a partition of comparable granularity the graded advantage is about 7%, not the 47% the figure implies. The direction survives; the magnitude does not. Quote 2.00 vs 1.87, not 2.00 vs 1.36.
  • Panel D's four bars are hard-coded literals in make_decomposition_figure.py — there is no spatial_coherence.csv behind them. The two that could be checked reproduce exactly (1.365 → 1.36, 2.003 → 2.00), so the numbers are right, but that panel can drift from the analysis without anything failing.
  • "K=7 sits just past the knee" describes a point that was never measured. The held-out sweep evaluated 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10…18 — it skips 7. K=7 is interpolated between K=6 (0.563) and K=8 (0.591).
  • K is no better determined than it was for k-means. Held-out variance explained rises monotonically from 0.379 at K=2 to 0.667 at K=18 and never turns over. There is no optimum; K is a judgement about where the gain flattens. Anyone who asks "why seven?" is asking a fair question and the answer is not statistical.
  • The argmax is not the result. Whenever you see a hard label from this model — in MOBA, in a glassbrain, in a decoder — it is a projection that discards most of the signal. Component 2 has 97 electrodes that call it their leading component and 583 that carry it above 0.08. Never show an argmax map without the loading map beside it, which is why FIG C.3 B2 and B3 are drawn as a pair.
  • Loadings are not probabilities, and components are not orthogonal. A weight of 0.4 does not mean "40% type A", and two components can overlap in both time course and territory. Phrases like "this electrode is mostly component 3" are safe; percentages and exclusive claims are not.
  • Replication across patients is recognisable, not identical — components correlate r = 0.687 ± 0.036 between independent halves of the cohort. Strong enough to describe, not strong enough to claim a fixed set of canonical response types.
  • 24 patients cannot settle generalisation, by either method. Every stability number on this page is reported against a size-matched null precisely because the raw value on its own would be meaningless at this n.
  • The negative result is the durable part. If you take one thing from this section: partitions of this dataset are not reproducible across preprocessing, have low silhouette at every K, and force a confident label on the 59% of electrodes that do not have one. That stands whatever replaces them. The graded model is the better description currently available, not a settled answer.

Stage 05

Response timing — when do the groups respond, in seconds?

The question the warp cannot answer

Every analysis before this one runs on time-normalised trials. 140 warps each trial to 300 bins so that 0% is stimulus onset and 50% is the GO cue. That makes trials comparable, but it also means a latency can only ever be expressed as a percentage of a trial whose real duration was thrown away. Both the clustering (02) and the pooling roles (04) live in that space.

This stage reads the GO-aligned real-time cubes from 150_ERSP_analysis_pipeline_noTwarping.ipynb and asks: in seconds after the GO cue, in what order do those already-defined groups respond, and does the order survive the move from one condition to another?

Method

  • Input. 05_ERSP_LM_RAWONLY_RealTime/<pid>/LM/ERSP_matrix/<cond>/ <pid>_<cond>_<ref>_ERSP_<chan>_RT_GO.npy, shape (129, T). 9241 cubes, 29 patients.
  • Neither axis is stored in the file. Both are rebuilt in 05_FBM_ResponseTiming/functions/lf_rt.py from the expressions the pipeline itself used — x = linspace(-2.5, 5.0, T, endpoint=False) and f = linspace(0, 500, 129) — and that module is the only place this knowledge lives. t = 0 is the GO cue, i.e. stimulus offset.
  • T varies from 369 to 635 across the tree because the window is fixed but the sampling is not, so the cubes differ in time resolution, not extent. Everything is resampled onto one 20 ms grid — the pipeline's own STFT hop — before anything is compared.
  • Timing features are measured on the mean 70–150 Hz trace over 0 → 5 s after GO: onset latency (first crossing of +1 dB sustained ≥ 100 ms), peak latency, peak amplitude, centre of mass, suprathreshold duration, positive area, and the mirrored suppression versions. Values are dB against the pre-stimulus baseline, so 0 dB is baseline and the thresholds are absolute. A fixed pre-GO reference would sit in silence for picture (1 s stimulus) but inside the stimulus for reading (3.5 s) and would not mean the same thing in the two.
  • Join. Each electrode is matched to every group label it already carries — k-means and Ward clusters on both feature sets, the convex-NMF leading component and its full weight vector, and the pooling role — on patient + punctuation-stripped contact name.

Why Kruskal–Wallis is the wrong test here

Electrodes within a patient share a brain, a reference, a montage and a response speed, so they are nowhere near independent. Pooled across electrodes, Kruskal–Wallis returns p < 1e-10 for all twelve group × condition cells — including ones the honest test calls null. It is measuring cohort composition, not physiology.

The headline test instead shuffles group labels within each patient, asking whether membership still orders electrodes in time given that patient's own contacts. It matters: the patient's median response duration correlates with electrode latency at ρ = 0.20–0.48, significant in 5 of 6 condition × feature cells (RT-T5).

The p in langloc_n806_p_0.001 / 0.01 / 0.05 is the individual-subject significance threshold used when defining each person's activation before overlap was computed. So there are two independent axes: which file (how strictly each individual's language map was defined) and which voxel-value cutoff (how much cross-subject agreement you demand). The p=0.2 / 0.4 in the meeting sketch refers to the second one.

2

Sweep the threshold instead of guessing it

3

Correlate every TF bin against the continuous atlas value

  • The ERSP cube is (2644 contacts × 129 freq × 900 time), the three conditions stitched. For each of the 116 100 bins: take that bin's value across all contacts, correlate against their atlas values, keep the coefficient — then replot all coefficients back onto the TF plane.
  • Spearman (rank-based) by default: the atlas distribution is heavily zero-inflated and skewed, and ERSP values have outliers. Pearson is available as an option.
  • Computed in chunks over bins so the 2644 × 116 100 matrix is never rank-transformed all at once.
  • p from the standard t transform, then Benjamini–Hochberg FDR across all 116 100 bins — the multiple-comparison correction the notes flagged as open. Drawn as the black contour.
  • No threshold is applied here — which also means this analysis is immune to the multi-attribution problem that affects any hard region assignment.
4

  • The binary version (the meeting's Fig 6): cortical contacts above/below the cutoff, each branch averaged into a [audio | picture | reading] triptych. Produced at both p≥0.1 and p≥0.5, for all three files.

How to read these figures

Three different colour scales appear below and they mean different things — worth fixing before reading the results.

  • Green → yellow on a time–frequency panel = a CORRELATION (Fig 9). Yellow = closer to the language network means more power in that bin; dark green = less; mid-green ≈ none. Sequential scale, so the thin grey r = 0 line is what separates the two directions. The black contour outlines bins surviving BH-FDR q<0.05 — inside it the relationship is reliable; speckle outside it is noise.
  • Red / blue on an ERSP panel = POWER in dB (Fig Result 3), not a correlation. Red = a power increase relative to that electrode's own pre-stimulus baseline, blue = a decrease. Same colours, completely different quantity — check the colourbar label.
  • Viridis (dark purple → green → yellow) = the atlas value (Fig Result 2b): the proportion of the 806 subjects with language activation at that electrode's location. Purple ≈ 0, yellow ≈ 0.65 (the maximum reached at any of our contacts). Grey dots are contacts with a value of exactly 0.
  • Teal vs grey = a hard membership split (Fig Result 1b): teal is above the chosen cutoff, grey below.

Reading any time–frequency panel: x is the warped trial (0–100% within each of the three condition blocks, dashed line = the GO-cue at 50%, so left of it is perception and right of it is production); y is frequency 0–500 Hz, with the 70–150 Hz high-gamma band the one that indexes local neural firing. The three blocks are the same electrodes doing the task three ways, so a feature present in one block but not the others is modality-specific, and one present in all three is modality-general.

How this relates to the LanA / Fedorenko work

Worth being explicit about what is being compared, because the two studies measure different things and one of their central findings directly predicts the size of our effects.

  • What the atlas is a map of. LanA was built from a validated language localiser (sentences > nonwords) in 806 people; in each person the top 10% most localiser-responsive voxels were taken, and the atlas value is how often a location fell in that set. So it maps where language-responsive BOLD overlaps across people — not where language "is" in any one brain.
  • Their headline motivation predicts our ceiling. The whole reason LanA exists is that language regions vary substantially in location between individuals — group averaging blurs them, which is why the field moved to subject-specific localisers. We are correlating individual electrodes against a group probabilistic map, so that same inter-individual variability is a built-in ceiling on our correlations. |r| ≤ 0.16 is what that framework would lead you to expect, not a sign the analysis failed. The honest conclusion is about the structure of the map, not the magnitude.
  • Where we agree. Our in-network contacts land perisylvian — superior/middle temporal and inferior frontal — and are left-dominant (621 of 1042), reproducing the canonical frontotemporal, left-lateralised layout without being told to. That is a genuine convergence, from a different modality.
  • What we add that fMRI cannot. LanA has no temporal resolution. Our map puts the language-network gradient on a millisecond-scale time–frequency plane and shows it is positive during the spoken prompt and during production, but negative during visual stimulus encoding — a within-trial dissociation that a BOLD atlas is not able to express.
  • The important caveat in comparing them. BOLD language-responsiveness and high-gamma language-responsiveness are not guaranteed to mark the same tissue. Agreement is evidence; disagreement would not automatically mean either is wrong.
  • An available refinement. LanA can be filtered by demographic variables including network lateralisation and handedness. Restricting to the typically-lateralised subset would sharpen the group map and may raise the correlation ceiling — a concrete next step if the effect sizes matter for the write-up.

Lipkin et al. (2022), Scientific Data — "Probabilistic atlas for the language network based on precision fMRI data from >800 individuals".

Honest caveats

  • Effect sizes are small — |r| ≤ 0.16. These clear FDR because n=2644, not because the effects are large. Report the map's structure, not the coefficient magnitudes.
  • Coverage is not random. Electrodes are placed for clinical reasons; the atlas-value distribution at our contacts is whatever that sampling produced, not a controlled design.
  • fMRI-derived, applied to iEEG. LanA marks where the BOLD language response overlaps across subjects; it does not guarantee the same tissue shows the electrophysiological effect.
  • Adjacent bins are not independent — the STFT couples time and frequency resolution, so FDR across bins is conservative in intent but the bins it corrects over are correlated.
  • The binary split is the weaker framing, for the dilution reason above.

The three algorithms

The question. What is the actual difference between convex NMF, k-means and Ward?

All three take the same electrodes and put them into groups. What differs is what a group is allowed to be, and how an electrode joins one.

k-means. Every electrode belongs to exactly one group. A group is summarised by the average of its members, and an electrode joins whichever average it is closest to. Move one electrode and the average moves, so it re-checks, and repeats until nothing moves.

Ward. Same rule — one electrode, one group — but built the opposite way round. Start with every electrode alone, then repeatedly merge the two groups whose merger costs the least extra spread, and stop when K groups are left. Because it is a merge history, you can cut it at any K without refitting.

Convex NMF. An electrode does not have to pick. It gets a weight on every component — 0.7 of one and 0.3 of another is a legal answer, and a real one. And each component is itself built out of real electrodes rather than being a free point in space.

The trap this figure is built to spring: all three are shown on the same twelve electrodes, and all three put electrode e5 in the wrong place, in the same way. That is not three bugs. It is what happens when a real electrode genuinely sits between two response types and the method is only allowed to name one of them.

_half1 and _half2

The question. What are the _half1 / _half2 files, and why did they break the cohort?

Each electrode's trials are split into two halves and each half is averaged separately. If the two halves look like each other, the response is real. If they do not, it was noise that happened to average into a shape.

So a half is a check on an electrode, not an extra electrode. There is no third recording site; there is one site whose trials were counted twice in two piles.

What went wrong. The halves were being written next to the full cubes, and the loader globs ERSP_matrix for cubes. It found three files per electrode and believed all three. The cohort went from 9,342 real cubes to 19,380 rows, and the responsiveness gate — which each half passes easily, because a half of a good electrode is still good — went from 35.1% to 96.9% without anything failing.

The fix was to move the files, not to filter them: halves now live in ERSP_halves/, and nothing that globs ERSP_matrix can see them. Filtering by filename would have worked until the next naming convention.

What stability means

The question. What does sweep_stability actually measure?

Not is the fit good. It measures would I get the same clusters again.

It refits the same K many times on random 80% subsamples of the electrodes, and for every pair of electrodes asks: of the rounds where both were drawn, how often did they land in the same cluster? A pair only counts in a round where both were present — otherwise sitting out would look like disagreement.

Each cluster's score is the average of that number across its own members. 1.0 means every pair stayed together every time it could have. 0.5 means the cluster is not a stable object. The number worth reading is the minimum across clusters, not the mean — one unstable cluster is the finding, and averaging hides it.

Why the cNMF stability number was wrong

The question. The stability figure quoted for the convex-NMF runs was about half what it should be. What was it actually measuring?

The resampler above refits each subsample and asks who lands together. The question is refits it with WHAT.

It always refitted with k-means. On a k-means or Ward run that is right by definition. On a convex-NMF run it was measuring how reproducibly k-means partitions the space the decomposition was fitted in — which is a different question — and it did it in the wrong space as well, raw dB instead of the unit-normed space convex NMF actually fits in.

Refitting with the run's own method changes the answer by a lot. At the published K on concat_hg: 0.658 natively against 0.332. The old number sits below Hennig's 0.60 rule of thumb, which reads as “not a real cluster”; the native one sits above it. Ward was understated too, by about 0.07.

The check that makes this a correction rather than just a different number: on a k-means run the native refit IS the old refit, so the two must agree exactly — and they do, bit-identical at every K on both feature sets. If they had not, the new estimator would have been wrong somewhere and the disagreement on the cNMF runs would have proved nothing.

The old column is kept rather than overwritten. It is what was published, and a number that silently changes meaning is worse than a number that is wrong next to a number that is right.

Three easy confusions

The question. Threshold vs argmax, convex NMF vs semi-NMF, graded vs convex — what is the difference?

All three are about the same object: the loading matrix a graded model returns, one row per electrode, one column per component.

1 — argmax vs threshold. Argmax reads each row and keeps only its largest entry, which turns the graded answer back into a partition: every electrode in exactly one cluster, the gradedness thrown away at the last step. Threshold keeps every entry above some level, so an electrode can be in two clusters or in none. Argmax is what the current figures do, and it is why a graded model can end up reported as if it were k-means.

2 — convex NMF vs semi-NMF. Both come from the same paper. Semi-NMF factorises X ≈ F G′ with only G forced non-negative — F is free, so a component can be any shape at all. Convex NMF adds one more constraint: X ≈ G(W′X) with W ≥ 0, which forces each component to be a weighted average of real electrodes. Semi-NMF is graded without being convex.

3 — graded vs convex. These get used as if they were the same word and they are not. Convex is about the COMPONENT: is it made of real data? Graded is about the ELECTRODE: may it belong partly to several? k-means is already convex — its centroid is literally the mean of its own members, which is a convex combination of real electrodes. So convexity is not what convex NMF buys over k-means. Gradedness is.

The spectral feature sets

The question. What is the difference between concat_rawds, concat_bands5 and concat_bands5z?

All three describe the same electrodes over the same three conditions. They differ in how finely the spectrum is cut, and whether the bands are put on an equal footing.

concat_rawds — 15 bands × 3 conditions × 30 bins = 1350 features. The published set.

concat_bands5 — the same spectrum in 5 bands (1–20, 20–70, 70–170, 170–270, 270–400 Hz), 450 features. Every edge lands on a 15-band edge, so the two are nested. It is statistically indistinguishable from all 15 on anatomical coherence (p = 0.35 over 6 seeds) at a third of the width. Four bands is measurably worse, so the 170 | 270 split is doing real work.

concat_bands5z — the same five, each z-scored to equal weight. Units are SD within a band, not dB. Euclidean distance has no idea 1/f exists: the 1–20 Hz band alone holds 44% of the sum of squares, so the fit spends most of its budget on low-frequency power whether the structure is there or not.

Normalisation is the biggest lever in the whole feature definition. Raw against z-scored gives ARI 0.37 on the same electrodes at the same K — a larger change than swapping k-means for Ward or convex NMF, which agree at 0.25–0.36.

Spearman or Pearson

The question. What is the difference between Spearman and Pearson for skewed data?

Both answer “do these two go up together?”, but they read different things. Pearson works on the values and measures how close the cloud is to a straight line. Spearman throws the values away, replaces each with its rank, and runs Pearson on those.

So Spearman asks only does y go up when x goes up, never by how much. On skewed data that is usually the safer question, because one far-out value can drag a straight line a long way but is worth exactly one rank.

Rule of thumb: if the question is “do they go up together”, use Spearman. If the SHAPE or the SIZE of the change is the thing being measured — a waveform, a lag, a slope — use Pearson. Panel D lists where each is used in this project and why; lf_atlas_corr defaults to Spearman and its docstring gives the reason.

The plan — from five types to a graded decomposition

The diagnostic pass above says a five-type taxonomy is not supportable as it stands. This is the replacement, and the point of it is a figure sequence that argues one thing: language cortex here is better described as a small number of overlapping response profiles that electrodes mix than as discrete types each electrode belongs to. The negative result becomes load-bearing rather than a footnote.

Why change at all — three findings, all recomputed from run 20260803_175417.
  • The partition is a preprocessing choice. Agreement between solutions from five defensible pipelines, same data, same K, same seed: raw vs unit-norm ARI 0.17, vs PCA-whitened 0.05. Not variations on one answer — different answers.
  • K is not identifiable. Silhouette K=4/5/6 = 0.166 / 0.176 / 0.171, a spread smaller than seed noise; under 80% subsampling P(K=4 > K=5) = 0.88.
  • Hard labels fight the data. 19% of sampled cortex has no cluster above 50% of the local mix; in the per-condition runs 375 electrodes take a different cluster for audio than for reading.

Method — convex NMF, which has direct precedent on this exact data type

Convex non-negative matrix factorisation on high-gamma time courses, giving every electrode a graded weight on every component instead of one label. Crucially cNMF admits signed data — the earlier objection that baseline-relative dB blocks NMF was wrong.

  • Hamilton, Edwards & Chang (2018), Current Biology 28(12):1860–1871 — 10.1016/j.cub.2018.04.033. Origin of cNMF-on-high-gamma in speech iEEG; continuous per-electrode weights across 27 participants.
  • Hamilton, Oganian, Hall & Chang (2021), Cell 184(18):4626–4639 — 10.1016/j.cell.2021.07.019. cNMF on z-scored, signed high-gamma. Conclusion is explicitly parallel-and-distributed rather than hierarchical — the same shape of claim this plan would make.
  • Kurteff et al. (2024), J. Neurosci. — 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1109-24.2024. cNMF on three conditions epoched and concatenated — structurally our matrix.
  • Norman-Haignere et al. (2022), Current Biology 32(7):1470–1484 — 10.1016/j.cub.2022.01.069. Electrode × concatenated-timecourse matrix; every electrode gets a graded weight on every component.

Secondary: archetypal analysis, which describes electrodes as convex mixtures of extreme profiles and so separates specialists from generalists directly. Olsen et al. (2022), Front. Neurosci. 16:911034 — 10.3389/fnins.2022.911034 — is the closest architectural template: scale- and polarity-invariant AA on unit-norm data, replacing hard microstate labels with continuous mixtures, with an archetype-generating matrix shared across subjects and subject-specific mixing weights. Hinrich et al. (2016), IEEE JSTSP 10(7):1160–1171 — 10.1109/JSTSP.2016.2595103 — is the multi-subject formulation. Cona et al. (2019), NeuroImage 185:322–334 — 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2018.10.050 — supplies the specialist/generalist framing in humans.

Validation — replacing silhouette with statistics this field accepts

  • Component count by cross-validated prediction accuracy — Norman-Haignere 2022 (above): "used cross-validated prediction accuracy to determine the number of components". Held-out electrodes, not internal compactness.
  • Discovery / replication split — Yeo et al. (2011), J. Neurophysiol. 106(3):1125–1165 — 10.1152/jn.00338.2011: independent halves clustered separately, 97.4% of vertices assigned to the same network.
  • Spatial agreement by Dice — Gordon et al. (2016), Cereb. Cortex 26(1):288–303 — 10.1093/cercor/bhu239: Dice 0.71 between boundary maps from independent datasets.
  • Consensus across preprocessing pipelines — Lowe & Schall (2018), eNeuro 5(5) — 10.1523/ENEURO.0131-18.2018: consensus clustering over 48 pipelines (6 scalings × 4 measurements × 2 metrics). This is the published answer to our ARI-0.17 problem: do not pick a pipeline, cluster the consensus.
  • Leave-one-subject-out — Huang et al. (2023), J. Neurosci. — 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1325-22.2023: the one iEEG clustering paper using LOSO. Every such number reported here will carry a size-matched pseudo-group null beside it, because that null already overturned one of our own findings.
  • Keep the K sweep — Castellucci et al. (2026), Cell Reports — 10.1016/j.celrep.2025.116783: 22 patients, 1,784 electrodes, K swept 2–10 across four algorithms. Closest published match to this study's shape.
  • Continuous description has iEEG precedent too — Banks et al. (2023), PLoS Biology 21(8):e3002239 — 10.1371/journal.pbio.3002239: diffusion map embedding on 6,742 intracranial sites from 49 participants.

How the decomposition actually decides — and what drives each component

Both figures below exist to answer one question that FIG C.3 states but does not let you see: convex NMF never partitions anything. It fits K additive profiles and gives every electrode a weight on each, X ≈ G(W′X) with W, G ≥ 0. The cluster label used everywhere else on this site — in the glassbrains, the centroids, the timing ladder — is an argmax over G taken afterwards, outside the model. C.5 is what that step throws away. C.6 asks whether throwing it away changes the answer.

Reading C.7 — unit-norm, argmax, and what “its own space” means

Three terms carry most of the weight in FIG C.7 and in FIG C.12 below, and the figure is easy to misread without them.

TermWhat it isWhy it matters here
unit-norm Divide every electrode's 900-number vector by its own length, so all electrodes become the same length and only the shape of the response is left. Per-electrode vector norms run 8.96 to 126.13 — a 14× range. In raw dB the biggest difference between two electrodes is usually how loud they are, so a distance-based method sorts on loudness first. Unit-norm divides that out and asks about response shape instead.
argmax Convex NMF gives each electrode seven weights summing to 1 — how much of each component it expresses. The argmax is simply which weight is largest, used as a label so the result can be compared against k-means and Ward. It is a lossy summary of a graded quantity. Median top weight 0.43 against 0.14 for an even split; only 34% have a majority component; 236 electrodes (19%) have their top two within 0.05. For about a fifth of electrodes the label is close to a coin flip.
its own space The coordinate system a method was actually fitted in. Convex NMF fits on unit-normed data; k-means and Ward fit on raw dB. Nothing else differs — same matrix, same gate, same time warping. Silhouette is not a property of a clustering alone, but of the clustering and the coordinate system it is measured in. Scoring all three in dB measures two of them at home and one in a space it never optimised in.

Why this is not a technicality. Read each method at home and in the other's space, and the pattern is symmetric:

scored in dBscored in unit-norm
convex NMF (home: unit-norm)0.046  z = +10.094  z = +11
k-means (home: dB)0.113  z = +10−0.004  z = −25
Ward (home: dB)0.115  z = +15−0.010  z = −11

Every method separates decisively at home and collapses in the other's space. They are describing different structure, not contradicting each other about the same structure — which is also why they agree so weakly with one another (ARI 0.21 / 0.19 / 0.49). The first version of C.7 scored all three in dB and concluded that convex NMF does not separate; that was an error in the test, corrected 2026-08-19.

Which space is “correct”? Neither, in the abstract. Raw dB keeps amplitude, so part of what k-means separates is loud electrodes from quiet ones. Unit-norm divides amplitude out and compares shape only. The choice turns on whether amplitude is signal or nuisance here — and note the dB values are already relative to a pre-stimulus baseline, so “amplitude” means response amplitude, not raw power. The 14× spread is 14× in responsiveness, which is precisely why an amplitude gate and a distance metric end up measuring nearly the same thing (FIG C.10).

The same three methods, on twelve electrodes you can read

Next — replacing the amplitude gate with a reliability gate

The single clearest gap between this project and both papers is how contacts are chosen. Ours are selected by amplitude (n_high_activity), keeping 1267 of 2946. Theirs are selected by reproducibility. An amplitude threshold cannot separate a small-but-repeatable response from a large-but-random one, and FIG C.10 already showed that the 1679 contacts our gate removes are not a different response type — they carry the same feature signature (r = 0.75–0.95), only weaker.

Why this could not be measured until now. compute_ersp built the per-trial stack and then collapsed it; the trial axis existed only inside the function and was never written to disk. Every saved cube is already trial-averaged, so no split-half estimate was possible from any product in the tree.

What was changed (2026-08-21), additively. compute_ersp now also returns avg_db_h1 and avg_db_h2 from an odd/even split of the same per-trial stack, and notebooks 140 and 150 write them as <stem>_half1.npy / _half2.npy beside the cube. avg_db is untouched — verified bit-identical against the pre-change code. Odd/even rather than first-half/second-half so that drift, fatigue and block structure are balanced across the halves instead of being confounded with reliability; Norman-Haignere split on odd and even runs for the same reason. The halves are deliberately not NaN-filled, unlike the cube: filling imputes the same bins in both halves from their own neighbours and would inflate the correlation for reasons that have nothing to do with the response.

  • Validated against answers known in advance, since nothing here can be checked after the fact without a multi-hour re-run: avg_db bit-identical (true); mean of the two halves equals avg_db (true); a planted repeatable response scores r = +0.908; a pure-noise channel scores r = +0.037, which NH's 0.2 threshold correctly discards; odd trial counts split 4/3 without error; a single-trial channel returns an all-NaN second half rather than raising.
  • Two bugs caught before the re-run, both otherwise invisible. The ERSP filename carries the raw channel name (A_L10) while labels.csv carries the normalised one (AL10), so the join between the two gates matched 21 of 838 contacts; normalising both sides the way the pipeline does matches all 2946. And a left merge leaves the gate column as object dtype, where ~ is bitwise-not over Python bools and silently returns −1/−2 instead of negating.
  • What the result will mean. The number to read is not how many contacts survive — it is the disagreement: contacts kept today that do not reproduce, and contacts discarded today that do. Those two counts are exactly what an amplitude threshold cannot see, and they determine whether the cohort behind every figure on this page is the right one.
  • It also unlocks a second thing. Norman-Haignere reuse split-half reliability to noise-correct their cross-validated variance explained (Fig 1E). With the halves on disk, our bi-cross-validation curve can be expressed as a fraction of explainable variance rather than of total variance — which turns the K choice from a relative comparison into an absolute one.
